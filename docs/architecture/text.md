# Text

Text in NSGo: how the core prepares, lays out and reports text, how fields edit it, and how selections run across texts. Read this before changing text styles, a `TextEngine`, `Input` or selection.

## Text and drawing

A platform's `TextEngine` runs on the engine goroutine in three phases. `Prepare` shapes the text once, `PreparedText.Measure` answers layout at any width, and `PreparedText.Layout` lays it out at its final size. The core releases prepared text when it changes or its node goes.

`Measure` returns what the platform's layout measured, fractions kept, and the core alone rounds it up to a device pixel ([layout](core.md#commit)), so a whole point does not add up to a point to every text on a 2x screen (`TestAMeasuredTextRoundsUpToADevicePixel`). CoreText puts a frame's lines on whole points, so Apple's heights are whole points, those it works out itself for a text cut to its line limit or empty as well; its widths, DirectWrite's sizes, Pango's sizes (in Pango units from `pango_layout_get_size`) and the web's keep their fractions.

The `TextLayout` travels in `ItemText`; the main thread draws it and releases the one it replaces. Apple's engine keeps fonts, style attributes and frame paths in bounded process-wide caches, and a prepared text keeps its measured size per width.

An ASCII text that fits on one line, without breaks, attachments, drawn decorations or a line height, is typeset once as a `CTLine` and drawn as one, where a framesetter typeset it again for every size asked and every frame: a table's cells prepare in half the time. Its size, baseline and alignment are the frame's (`TestOneLineMatchesTheFramesetter` compares pixels, lines and carets); texts beyond ASCII, whose runs may fall back to another font, and fonts with typographic leading, which a frame leaves out of its last line, stay on the framesetter. The web measures such texts on a canvas ([performance](performance.md#keeping-frames-cheap)).

The core keeps what texts measure per engine, as NSH's `MeasureCache` (`measure.go`): sizes keyed by a hash of what lays a text out, colors left out, and the width it wraps at, in two generations of 1024 entries. Identical texts, such as a list's rows or a value that comes back, measure once, and the words a min-content width measures are not prepared again. A text whose hash stays, a new color, lays nothing out ([commit](core.md#commit)).

macOS draws a text or canvas view into a content layer of its own, as NSH does: a plain `CALayer` below the view's sublayers, drawn by its delegate's `drawLayer:inContext:`. No view implements `drawRect:`, whose display pass recorded and replayed every changed text, and redrew a scroll view's texts at every scroll step. A content layer draws again when its content or size changes, and only while some of it can be seen; the rest wait until a batch, a scroll or a layout animation shows them.

Canvases that paint often draw on Core Animation's threads, and their animated arguments redraw only what they change ([canvas](canvas.md#animated-arguments)).

The core does what needs no engine, as NSH's does:

- `TextTransform` changes the case of each span's characters as they are joined.
- A text's own multiplier caps the user's text size (`NoFontScaling`, `MaxFontSizeMultiplier`).
- `TextAlignVertical` moves the content box down to the text's measured height.
- `AdjustsFontSizeToFit` bisects a scale, down to `MinimumFontScale`, at which the untruncated text fits the box and its line limit, as NSH's `Paragraph::fit`. Only the final layout uses that text; layout measures the text as written. Font-relative attachments (`Canvas.Em` and icons) take the selected scale too, including their baseline descent, while point sizes and padding stay as authored. Candidate attachment layouts have independent layout states, and only the selected geometry is applied before canvases record their drawings; the natural measurements remain available when the text grows again.
- `Text.OnTextLayout` runs after the commit with the lines a `LineLayout` reports: UTF-8 ranges, boxes in the content box, and the metrics of the font each line starts in.

The engines draw the rest:

- `Font.Size` is in points, the units of a view's size, on every engine. Pango's own sizes are typographic points it scales by its context's resolution, 96 dpi unless set, so Linux gives Pango absolute sizes (`pango_font_description_set_absolute_size`, `pango_attr_size_new_absolute`) and its editors' CSS pixels, which GTK does not scale (`TestFontSizesAreLogicalPixels`); the user's text size reaches it as `FontScale`, from `gtk-xft-dpi`.
- `EllipsizeMode`. CoreText and Pango truncate the last line's rest at its head, middle or tail. CoreText keeps the offered width when the cut replaces a soft wrap, and aligns the last line after truncating it. Clipping keeps the lines whole and cuts them at the box.
- DirectWrite, the web and mini programs trim tails themselves (or over the remaining text of the last line's paragraph on mini programs), and Android's `TruncateAt` a head or middle on one line only. There `AttributedText.CutRest` replaces the lines past the last kept one with the most characters around an ellipsis that fit a line (`CutToFit`). Windows trims its measuring layout at the kept lines' height limit (preserving the offered width when a soft wrap will be cut at head or middle and resetting the height limit afterwards); mini programs measure after applying the same last-line truncation that `Layout` draws; and HarmonyOS applies `MaxLines` on the Go side over its host's `measure` bridge (dropping paragraphs past the limit, capping wrapped height to `MaxLines` lines via cached host line-height probes, and keeping the offered width on soft wraps) without requiring the precompiled ArkTS host to pass `maxLines` to `MeasureText` until rebuilt with the OpenHarmony API 26 SDK.
- `AttributedText.Origin` maps the attachments a cut text kept back to the app's indices.
- `TextAlignJustify` is each engine's justification; Android's needs no newer API than NSGo's minimum.
- `TextStyle.RTL` is the base direction: CoreText's paragraph writing direction, DirectWrite's reading direction, a Pango context's base direction without auto direction, Android's text direction heuristic and CSS's `direction`. Start and end alignments follow it ([styles](styles.md)).
- `TextDecorationStyle`, `TextDecorationColor`, `TextDecorationThickness` and `TextUnderlineOffset`, the offset putting an underline's top below the baseline, both auto (the font's) unless set and their percentages of the font size. CoreText underlines solid, double, dotted and dashed lines of the font's thickness and place in a color; Apple's backend draws the other underlines and every strikethrough itself, as Windows and Android draw the lines a font or paint cannot, Linux those Pango lacks (dotted, dashed, a thickness or offset, styled strikethroughs) and the web through CSS.
- Ink past a text's box, its lines and its shadow, is drawn, as a browser draws it: Apple's content layer grows to the layout's reach (`Layout.ContentFrame`), unless the view clips, and keeps a larger frame from before, up to four times what it needs, unless its view is rounded (`apple.KeptContent`). Windows leaves unclipped text views uncontained and includes their ink reach (`textLayout.inkBounds`) in partial-frame damage and blend areas; Linux sizes its decoration and tinted Cairo nodes to the drawn lines (`textLayout.decorationBounds`, `textLayout.contentBounds`) while `GskShadowNode` expands for text shadows; Android keeps `NsgoView` and `TextNode` unclipped (`clipChildren` and `clipToPadding` off), applies text shadows to custom decorations, sizes tinted layers to `TextNode.inkBounds` and invalidates the parent when overflowing ink appears or clears.
- `FontVariant` turns on OpenType features in `Font`: small capitals and oldstyle, lining, tabular or proportional figures, a node's replacing its parent's (`FontVariantNormal` undoes one), as NSH's `fontVariant`. CoreText fonts carry them as feature settings, DirectWrite as a typography per variant, Pango as a font-features attribute, Android as a paint's feature settings and the web as `font-variant-caps` and `font-variant-numeric`; inputs and canvases take them too. A browser's canvas has no numeric variants, so the web draws tabular figures one by one, each centered in the widest figure's advance, and other figure forms as the font's default.
- `Language`, an inherited BCP 47 tag, picks the glyph forms of characters languages share, such as Han's Japanese and Chinese forms, the fallback fonts, and Korean word-based line breaking as CSS's `word-break: keep-all` does. The root's is the language the app shows (`Env.Language`), else its locale, so text and inputs that set none follow a switch of language at runtime; a span's own applies to its run. It travels in `Font.Language`, which the core's measurements and Apple's font cache key on: CoreText takes it as a run's `kCTLanguageAttributeName` (with BCP 47 `-u-lw-keepall` for Korean) and the language of `CTFontCreateUIFontForLanguage`, with macOS's and iOS's inputs setting the same language attribute on their editors and placeholders and `NSLineBreakStrategyHangulWordPriority` on Korean multiline paragraphs; DirectWrite takes it as the format's locale (used by both text and Windows' own editor) and `SetLocaleName` for a span in another, joining keep-all Korean words with `U+2060` under emergency breaking; Pango takes it as a language attribute (and `pango_attr_allow_breaks_new(FALSE)` on Korean words under word-char wrapping), with Linux's `GtkEntry`, `GtkTextView` buffer tags and placeholder `GtkLabel` taking the same; Android takes it as the paint's and `EditText`'s text locale (with `-u-lw-keepall` and API 33+ `LineBreakConfig.LINE_BREAK_WORD_STYLE_PHRASE` for Korean); the web sets `lang` on text, span and input elements (and the caret mirror) with `word-break: keep-all` for Korean; and mini programs pick language-appropriate CJK font stacks for both offscreen canvas measurement and the render layer, send `lang` on text runs and inputs, break Korean lines between words in their Go text engine, and set `word-break: keep-all` on Korean multiline inputs.
- `AttributedText.Shadow`, the block's shadow: a Core Graphics shadow, a CSS `text-shadow`, Direct2D's shadow effect over the recorded glyphs, a GSK shadow node, Android's shadow layer.

`FontFamily` is one family or a CSS list (`Inter, "Noto Sans SC", sans-serif`), read by `driver.FontFamilies`; a character a family lacks comes from the next that has it. CoreText takes the others as a cascade list, each entry restyled with the face's weight and slant, CSS and Pango take the list as it is, DirectWrite draws in the first installed family and falls back along the others through an `IDWriteFontFallback`, then the system's, and Android builds a `Typeface.CustomFallbackBuilder` chain on API 29+ from registered app fonts, installed `SystemFonts` families and the list's trailing generic fallback (selecting the first available family on API 28). CoreText's framesetter computes each line's metrics from its runs' faces alone, so Apple's backend enforces the first family's ascent, descent and leading as a strut, stacking adjusted lines itself so measurements, drawing, decorations, attachments, line boxes, carets and hit tests agree with CSS's strut.

Italic runs keep color emoji upright, in spans of their own (`driver.UprightEmoji`): emoji fonts have no italic face, and Chrome and Android would slant them where CoreText does not.

A text with no family, and the system's families in a list (`system-ui`, `sans-serif`), take the platform's UI font: CoreText's system font, Segoe UI on Windows, Android's default and, on Linux, the family of GTK's `gtk-font-name`, which a `GtkLabel` shows. Linux reads it on the main thread, measures and draws each text in the one it was prepared with, and has the app prepare its text again when the desktop changes it (`TestTextFollowsTheUIFont`). Its editor CSS resolves each system family to that same name, preserving its place in a fallback list, and reloads when the setting changes; an unset family inherits GTK's default (`TestEditorsFollowTheUIFont`).

The family `FontMonospace` is each platform's fixed-pitch font: CoreText's user fixed-pitch font, CSS's `ui-monospace` stack, Consolas on Windows, Pango's `monospace` on Linux, Android's `monospace`.

`nsgo.Fonts` registers the app's font files, paths under `resources/`, before the first view, and texts name them by family. `internal/platform/fontfile` reads each face's family, weight and slant from its `name` and `OS/2` tables for the engines that take a face without them:

| Platform | Registers with |
| --- | --- |
| macOS, iOS | `CTFontManagerRegisterGraphicsFont`, for the process |
| Web | A `FontFace` each in `document.fonts`, loaded before the engine starts |
| Windows | A DirectWrite font collection of the system's fonts and the app's, loaded from memory, which text formats take |
| Linux | fontconfig's `FcConfigAppFontAddFile`, before GTK starts |
| Android | `Typeface.createFromAsset`, kept by family; a style takes the nearest face and synthesizes the rest |

`Scope.RegisterFont` registers a TrueType or OpenType font's bytes as the app runs, as a TSX module's import does: the backend's text engine is a `driver.FontRegistrar`, and the tree prepares every text again, a text prepared before the font came once more though its string is the same, inputs measure their lines again, and canvases run `Draw` again ([canvas](canvas.md)). Apple registers the bytes with CoreText and drops the fonts it looked up; Windows makes its collection again with them; Linux and Android write them to the cache, fontconfig adding the file and Pango's font maps made again or told, Java making a `Typeface` of it; the web adds a `FontFace`, which loads after the call, so the engine prepares the text and records the canvases again once it has (`Engine.FontsChanged`).

`Text.Spans` style byte ranges over the text's style; offsets are UTF-8 bytes, Go's own, and platforms convert them. `Text.SpansFunc` gives the spans again when what it reads changes, their presses as `Spans` take them, prepared again only when they differ: an editor's row recolors as its highlighting arrives, a text view's as its search matches change. `Scope.MeasureText` measures a text in a font as a canvas does and tracks font changes in reactive functions, for a column fitted to its text or a monospaced font's advance. A press or long press on a span with a handler comes back with the byte offset under the point.

- A span's `Background`, `CornerRadius`, `BackgroundInsetTop` and `BackgroundInsetBottom` become its `TextBackground`. Each fragment starts with a box from the font's ascent to its descent. The insets take points or percentages of that height, and the radius rounds the resulting band. The paint stays behind selection and glyphs and changes no layout. NSGo adds vertical insets to NSH's full-height boxes for marker highlights. Native and mini-program painters leave out hanging spaces, and the web sizes a background image inside each inline fragment. HarmonyOS reads the native text’s range rectangles after layout; mini programs use the existing measured runs without splitting them for paint.
- A span whose `Visibility` is hidden is hidden in place, as CSS's on an inline box and NSH's `visibility: "hidden"` span: `TextSpan.Hidden`, laid out as visible characters, drawn in a transparent ink (`hiddenInk`) with no background, no link, and left out of copies (`driver.CopiedText`, through `SelectionSpans` on the backends that select themselves), of screen readers (a text's label is its shown text) and of `nsgotest`'s text selectors. The layout hash leaves it out, so a span showing or hiding lays nothing out. The web hides it with `visibility: hidden`.
- A span's `Attachment` is a view inside the text, as NSH's inline attachments are. Its view is a layout root at its content size, its content not stretched across the space it is laid out in, laid out before the text is prepared, so a change inside it prepares the text again. `SpansFunc` mounts each attachment in a scope of its own. When the spans run again, each attachment they hold again with the same classes and style keeps its view and its scope, matched by identity wherever it moves, and the text around it restyles; only the new ones are made, those gone are disposed, and the text's views follow the spans' order. A `Node` compares only as a pointer or a value of a comparable type (`sameNode`), so a `Func` or a `View`, which holds a slice, is never the same, and a caller that keeps an attachment gives the same pointer each time.
- The text holds an `AttachmentRune` in an attachment's place, and `AttributedText.Attachments` gives the engine its size, `VerticalAlign` and `Descent`. A baseline-aligned attachment uses the first line of its first visible text in flow. The core reads that baseline from the layout it will mount, so the text's fallback fonts and placement within the view count. An `Em` canvas ([canvas](canvas.md)) uses its declared baseline and takes the surrounding text's `NoFontScaling` and `MaxFontSizeMultiplier`. An attachment without a baseline uses its bottom. A change to size, alignment or descent prepares the surrounding text again.
- Every engine places an attachment by NSH's rule, `TextAttachment.Ascent` and `Extent` against the placeholder font's `FontExtent`, so a line grows where an attachment reaches past its font. The Go engines take `Descent` through `Ascent`; the web lowers the placeholder by a `vertical-align` length, and Android reads it from its `Prepare` message.
- An attachment span's `Content` is what a copy takes in its place (`TextSpan.Copy`, `driver.CopiedText`), as a formula copies as its TeX; without it a copy leaves the attachment out. Screen readers read the attachment's labels in its place instead (`driver.ShownText`), where a text reads its shown text for its label or a group reads its texts.
- After layout the core asks the `AttachmentLayout` where each attachment went and sends its view as a native child of the text view at that frame. Those cut off by the line limit are hidden.

A `Canvas` records its drawing on the engine into an immutable `Drawing` the platform plays ([canvas](canvas.md)).

## Android runtime

Android sends `Prepare`, `Measure`, `Layout`, `Lines` and `Carets` over the runtime pipe with a request ID separate from the prepared text or layout ID. Java always replies with data or an error for a request whose header it can read. Go checks the operation and ID, ignores duplicate or late replies, and waits at most one second for each text request, including its queued write. A failed measurement uses the approximate engine's size, and failed line and caret queries return no geometry. A failed native layout leaves its text and attachments without a native layout for that commit.

## Hit testing

A final layout answers where its carets are, as NSH's `TextEngine` hit testing does (`internal/driver/hittest.go`): `HitTest` gives the `Caret` nearest a point, `CaretRect` a line-high box of no width, and `RangeRects` the boxes a range covers, a box per run of one direction. Points are in the content box and offsets UTF-16. A point past the end of a wrapped line gives its end with `Upstream` affinity, so the caret stays on that line.

- Every engine's layout implements `driver.TextHitTester` through `driver.LazyCarets`: on first use it reads each line's box and its clusters (the characters one caret stops around, with their edges and direction) into a `CaretLayout`, and plain Go answers from then on, on either thread. Releasing the layout keeps what was read.
- The engines read clusters from their own layouts: CoreText's glyph runs, Pango's cluster iterator on a context of hit testing's own (Pango objects belong to one thread at a time), DirectWrite's `HitTestTextPosition`, the browser's character ranges in the measuring element, and Android's carets by grapheme in Java, sent over the pipe. The headless engine gives each character its approximate width.
- macOS and iOS selections hit-test and highlight through it.

## Text inputs

`Input` follows NSH's `<Input>`: one line, or several with `Multiline` in each platform's multi-line control (NSTextView, UITextView, `<textarea>`, GtkTextView, a multi-line EditText, and Windows' drawn editor wrapping its lines).

- A multiline input is measured with its value, or its placeholder while empty, wrapped at its width, so it grows unless its style sizes it. `OnContentSizeChange` is the core's: after a layout that changed the value's size, one empty line's for an empty input.
- In a macOS TextKit 2 input, a line taller than the font puts the extra height between line boxes and shifts the text origin to keep the baselines. The insertion indicator, selection and candidate anchor use the font-height boxes. TextKit 1 and the placeholder still use the full line height.
- A multiline input scrolls in its padding box (inside its border) with its text inset by `InputConfig.Padding`, so its scroll bar runs along the edges and its text scrolls under the top and bottom padding: `NSScrollView` + `NSTextView` on macOS, `UITextView` on iOS, `ScrollView` (`InputField.ScrollHost`) + `EditText` on Android, Direct2D padding-box clipping and overlay indicator on Windows, `GtkScrolledWindow` + `GtkTextView` margins on Linux, and `<textarea>` padding with `scroll-padding` on the web. HarmonyOS serializes `InputConfig.Padding` with its `"in"` op; its ArkTS host still renders a `TextInput` until rebuilt with the OpenHarmony API 26 SDK.
- `InputConfig.Submit` resolves `SubmitBehavior` with `Multiline` as NSH's `InputConfig::submit()`, and backends read only it: single lines blur and submit by default, multiline inputs break the line.
- Shift, Option or Alt with Return always break a multiline input's line; a Return that commits a composition never submits.
- The platform answers `MaxLength` and Return natively, without waiting for the engine; iOS and Android offer software Return to the current keymap before native submit, blur or newline handling ([keys](services.md#keys-and-the-status-bar)).
- `internal/platform/field` keeps what every Go backend reports the same way: edit counts, selections, keys, compositions, and a `Value` held while an input method composes. Android's `InputField` does the same in Java.
- An input that loses the focus while an input method composes ends the composition, `OnCompositionChange{Composing:false}`, before its `OnBlur` and keeps the composed text on every platform:
  - Windows completes the IMM32 composition (`CPS_COMPLETE`) as the field loses the focus, whether a click, the app (`Ref.Focus`, `Ref.Blur`), a modal or alert, hiding or UI Automation moves it, so the committed `OnChange` comes before the end and the blur. A field that turns disabled or read-only while composing commits first in the same way, before it stops taking text.
  - macOS commits any marked text before the editor resigns, turns disabled or read-only, leaves the tree, or its window resigns key or closes (`unmarkText` followed by `inputContext.discardMarkedText` to end the input method's session without dropping live-converted text), reporting any uncommitted `OnChange` and `OnCompositionChange{Composing:false}` before `OnBlur`. iOS keeps the editor's marked text as UIKit unmarks it while the editor resigns.
  - The web keeps what the browser commits as the element blurs: Chrome fires `compositionend` with the composed text in the value, and the backend reports it before the end.
  - Android's `InputField` clears the composing span and ends the composition before `View.onFocusChanged` invokes its focus listener (and before turning disabled or read-only), keeping its text and reporting any unreported `OnChange` and `OnCompositionChange{Composing:false}` before `OnBlur`.
  - On Linux the preedit is never in a GTK 4 editor's text, and the backend follows focus through a `GtkEventControllerFocus` added after the editor's own so GTK's `focus-out` notifies `GtkIMContext` first: whatever the input method commits reaches the field without duplication, and an uncommitted or dropped preedit is committed into the editor before `OnCompositionChange{Composing:false}` and `OnBlur` (and likewise before the editor turns disabled or read-only).
- Selections are UTF-16 offsets. `SelectedRange` and `Ref.SetSelection` send `ItemSelection` after the batch's value.
- `PlaceholderColor` and `SelectionColor` are inherited text styles. `CaretColor`, which is not, is the caret's and it takes the selection color without one; iOS tints both with it, and Android's runtime, which has one color for them, does too. An input without a `SelectionColor` takes its theme's `Selection` and, without a `CaretColor`, its `Accent`. An opaque `SelectionColor` highlights at 30%, a translucent one as it is (`InputConfig.Highlight`). `HideCaret` hides it. On macOS the focused input gives the app its theme's `Accent` through AppKit's private `_setAccentColor:`, where the system's accent is Multicolor, so input methods draw their candidates and marked text in it; a theme change reactivates the input context, the only point input methods read it at, unless they are composing. A host's app keeps its own accent.
- `OnCaretMove` reports the caret's bounds in the input while it has the focus, and the core adds them in the surface for `Overlay.AnchorBounds`. Each platform reads them from its own editor:

  | Platform | Caret bounds |
  | --- | --- |
  | macOS | AppKit's `firstRectForCharacterRange:actualRange:`, falling back to the input's own screen box for point-wide editor sinks and clipped ranges so input-method candidates open beside the caret rather than at the screen's corner |
  | iOS | UIKit's caret rect, even with `HideCaret` |
  | Android | The EditText's `Layout` after it takes the text |
  | Linux | GTK's cursor extents and locations |
  | Windows | NSGo's own editor |
  | Web | A hidden copy of the field holding its text up to the caret |

- `Input.Suggest` follows a trigger query in the field, as mentions after `@`, for a list beside it such as `base.SuggestList` ([mention completion](mention-completion.md)): the text after the trigger, with no space unless `AllowSpaces`, up to a caret with no selection, the trigger at a line's start or after a space, or after one of `AllowedPrefixes` in the space's place. The core feeds it the field's edits, selections, caret bounds, focus and compositions from `Dispatch` in the field's owner, where its `OnOpen` and `OnClose` run. A value the app sets, such as a draft cleared on sending, and the field losing the focus or leaving the tree, which hears no blur, end the query. Escape, `Close` and `Accept` dismiss the trigger they close, as `base/richeditor`'s `Suggestion` does: the query stays closed on it, its offset moved by edits before it, however the query grows or the caret moves within it, until the match ends or a trigger starts elsewhere, so an insert that starts with the trigger, such as `@alice`, does not reopen it as the platform reports the caret the insert moved. `Composing` reports an input method's composition, during which a list leaves the keys to it, as `Editor` does. `Accept(text)` replaces the trigger and query by UTF-16 offsets, sets an uncontrolled field's text itself and a controlled one's through `OnChange`, and sends the caret after the insert.
- `OnPaste` reports what the user pasted, read as they paste: the text, HTML when the clipboard holds it (`public.html` on macOS and iOS, `CF_HTML` on Windows, `text/html` on Linux and the web, `ClipData.Item.getHtmlText` on Android), local files as `file:` URLs, else an image's bytes. `PasteBehavior` `PasteReport` keeps the text out; images and files never go in.
- Pastes are caught where each platform pastes. macOS gives its fields a field editor of NSGo's own (the window's delegate hands it out) and its text views their own `paste:`; iOS overrides `paste:`, clipping pastes for inputs with `MaxLength` (or single-line / submitting-multiline rules) on a composed character sequence boundary before calling the delegate and `insertText:` so UIKit's undo manager records the clipped edit instead of crashing on the next undo; Android overrides `onTextContextMenuItem`.
- Linux hears `paste-clipboard` and reads GDK's clipboard asynchronously, so its report comes after the text it inserts, as on the web for files, whose bytes are read first.
- What a platform lacks is a no-op there. Return keys, capitalization and the keyboard's appearance belong to software keyboards: iOS, Android, the web's `enterkeyhint` and `autocapitalize`; Linux passes GTK input hints. `IOS.KeyboardAppearance` is iOS's only.
- `AutoComplete` maps to iOS's `textContentType`, Android's autofill hints, the web's `autocomplete`, GTK's input purpose and macOS's content types for accounts, passwords and codes. macOS keeps AutoFill's guesses to masked fields without `AutoComplete`: a guess at a plain field can flash an empty panel over it. Windows has no autofill or autocorrection.

## Text selection

Selection follows NSH's: one selection runs across the texts of a selection area (a `View` with `Selectable`, NSH's selectable views), in document order, as a web page's does. A `Text` with `SelectableOn` outside any area is an area of its own, which iOS hands to UIKit as NSH does; one with `SelectableOff` stays out. The highlight is the inherited `SelectionColor`, else the platform's.

- `ViewProps` carries `SelectionArea` (the view's `Selectable`), `Selectable` and `SelectionColor`, so a selection never waits for the engine.
- An area with `OnTextSelection` hears `EventSelectionChange` with the selected text as a copy gives it (`SelectionModel.Text`, the selection's `toString` on the web) and the box of the texts its selection touches (`SelectionModel.Extent`, the range's box on the web), each time either changes: the box stays while the selection moves within one text. macOS, iOS, Linux, Windows and the web also report what each text has selected, by its node and UTF-16 offsets (`Ranges`, from `SelectionModel.Ranges` or the browser's DOM ranges), and report again when only those change, as when the same words are chosen on another text. From those ranges and each node's `SelectionBlock` / `SelectionBlockFunc` (set by `base/textview` on its parsed blocks and inlines, or inferred from plain `Text` spans), `core` slices and serializes `TextSelection.Markdown` and `TextSelection.HTML` across partial spans, nested containers and multiple `TextView`s in one selectable area. Android does not report `Ranges` yet, falling back to plain text.
- `SelectionModel` in `selection.go` is the platform-independent model the Go backends drive on their main thread; `internal/platform/textselect` holds what Windows and Linux share of it. Android's `Selection.java` follows it on the UI thread.
- A backend lists an area's texts as `SelectionTexts`, with content, frames, hit test and NodeIDs, then feeds it presses, drags and long presses and draws its `Ranges`.
- Offsets are UTF-16. `Text` joins texts on one row with a tab and others with a line break, attachments as their spans' `Copy` or left out.
- macOS takes mouse presses in an area before its views see them and shows the I-beam over its texts; fields and gestures keep theirs. A press on a button or a link presses it unless it drags 10 points first, then selects, as NSH's does. The surface becomes first responder for Copy, Select All and Escape, and implements `NSServicesMenuRequestor` (`validRequestorForSendType:returnType:`, `writeSelectionToPasteboard:types:`) so macOS Services see the selection as plain text. A secondary click or Control-click over a selection without an app `OnContextMenu` opens AppKit's read-only selection menu—Look Up, Translate, Copy, Share, Speech and Services—built through a hidden non-editable `NSTextView` helper sized to the selection's highlight, and a three-finger tap opens `showDefinitionForAttributedString:atPoint:`.
- Windows and Linux select as macOS does, with drags, double clicks for words, triple clicks for a text and Shift to extend, and answer Ctrl+C, Ctrl+A and Escape; each draws the highlight under the glyphs.
- iOS selects in an area with a long press anywhere in it, buttons and links too, but on fields, gestures and views with a long press of their own, then by words as the finger drags, and shows the edit menu over the selection; the view controller answers Copy and Select All. Handles drawn as UIKit's, a 16-point knob in the selection's color made opaque where NSH's is 11 points in the tint, move the ends and take touches 22 points around their bars, which the area's long press and tap leave to them and the scroll views around wait for. A tap on the selection shows or hides the menu, and one off it clears the selection.
- A selectable text outside an area adopts `UITextInput` and a non-editable `UITextInteraction`, as NSH's does: UIKit selects, draws the highlight and handles in its tint (the `SelectionColor` made opaque) and shows its menu, Look Up, Translate and Share included. The text takes touches near its selection's ends, where the knobs stand out of it, and a touch going down outside it ends the selection, as one outside an area does.
- Android selects a word with a long press, as its text views do, with the system's handles and a floating toolbar for Copy, Select all and installed apps answering `ACTION_PROCESS_TEXT` (`text/plain`, `EXTRA_PROCESS_TEXT_READONLY = true`), Translate among them; the runtime and AAR manifests declare the `<queries>` entry API 30 and later require for package visibility. It uses Java's `BreakIterator` for word boundaries. A JVM fixture compares selection ranges, copied text and selection change reports with Go on the same text boxes and UTF-16 caret positions and checks `ACTION_PROCESS_TEXT` filtering, toolbar items and intent launches.
- The web uses the browser's own selection through `user-select`, and a `copy` handler joins texts as the model does and puts each attachment's copy text (`data-copy`) in its place. Android's optional mount footer carries copy and hidden-span ranges in UTF-16 independently of its cached layout; `TextCopy` applies the same replacement and omission rules to the toolbar, selection reports and `ACTION_PROCESS_TEXT`. Earlier runtimes ignore the footer until rebuilt. Unlike NSH v0.6.1, which omits attachments, this matches NSGo's shared selection model's copy text.
