# Scrolling and lists

How scroll views behave and report, and how `List` windows, measures, anchors and pins its rows. Read this before changing `Scroll`, `List` or a backend's scroll view.

## Scrolling

A scroll view follows NSH's `scrolling-and-lists.md`: `ScrollBehavior` holds paging, snapping, deceleration, bounces, the content inset and the keyboard props of `Scroll` and `List`, and `ScrollConfig` carries it with refreshing and zooming in `ItemScrollConfig`.

- Offsets are UIKit's `contentOffset`: a `ContentInset` makes them negative at the start. `ContentOffset` is an `ItemScrollTo` sent after the first commit.
- `Ref.Reveal` (NSH's `reveal`) brings a view into view: at the commit, with its layout, the core turns it into an `ItemScrollTo` of each scroll view around the view, innermost first, each clamped to its content, the view's box followed out through the offset it sets, and up to an overlay, a modal or a fixed view around it, which no scroll view outside it moves. The alignments are physical, `ScrollAlignStart` vertically and `ScrollAlignNearest` horizontally by default; `Animated` defaults to false, Go's zero value, where NSH's defaults to true. `Ref.RevealBox` reveals a box within the view the same way, from the box rather than the view's frame, as a search reveals a match in a long line of code through the code's horizontal scroll view; NSH has none. `Margin` is kept clear at the outermost scroll view's edges, for a bar over it.
- Every platform reports `EventScrollEnd` where a scroll view comes to rest: a drag that set nothing in motion ends, or a glide, a snap, an animated scroll or the wheel's turns do, not a glide a new drag catches. iOS hears `scrollViewDidEndDragging:willDecelerate:` without deceleration, `scrollViewDidEndDecelerating:` and `scrollViewDidEndScrollingAnimation:`; the web the DOM's `scrollend`; Android's `Scrolling.java` its drags, glides and eased scrolls; the desktops their own motion's ends (`restScroll`, macOS's `scrollEnded`); `nsgotest` its scroll steps' and drags' ends. Windows and Linux share snap initiation, scroll-end ordering and frame-coalesced offset reports in `internal/platform/scrollbar`. The snapping diff clamps settle there too, and `OnScrollEnd` of `Scroll` and `List`, TSX's `onScrollEnd`, hears it.
- `ScrollBehavior.SnapTarget` decides where a release snaps, as NSH's `snap_target`. iOS and macOS call it, Windows and Linux through `internal/platform/scrollbar`'s `Settle`; Android's `Scrolling.java` ports it, and the web marks `SnapPoints` for CSS scroll snapping.
- `OnScroll` runs at most once a turn, and at most once per `ScrollEventThrottle` when set (NSH's `scrollEventThrottle`): the core holds an offset that comes sooner and reports the latest when the interval has passed, on the graph's clock. The platforms report every move, which the core's hit tests need.
- Every scroll view reports its offset as it moves, so the core's hit tests and touches find what shows under a point. Anchored overlays follow immediate scroll commands and List compensation in the same batch. They follow an animated scroll's reported positions, not its target.
- Platforms report drags, momentum and refreshes with the offset and the zoom scale (`Event.Value`); the core adds the content and viewport sizes for `ScrollEvent`, and `Stale` while a scroll it sent is not shown applied: every report carries the count of `ItemScrollTo` and `ItemScrollBy` items applied (`Event.Count`), and one from before the latest is not where the user moved the content. After `EventRefresh` the config goes out again, so a spinner the app did not keep stops.
- A decorated Apple scroll view clips its content separately from its own outer shadows and outline, with a mask for differing corner radii. On macOS it follows committed scroll bounds and keeps the radius in viewport points while zooming; on iOS a holder follows the UIScrollView viewport with matching frame and bounds origins, holding its zoom view when present. Native scrolling and hit testing keep their content coordinates.
- iOS uses UIScrollView's own props and UIRefreshControl, and pulls an offset back to the content end after a resting content or viewport size change. Android uses its scroll views with OverScroller's spline glides (friction scaled by `DecelerationRate`) and pull, and the web CSS. macOS keeps [offsets of its own](#macos-scroll-views) and ignores the keyboard props.
- Android's vertical and horizontal views stop their own glide at dispatch of `ACTION_DOWN`, before a child can consume it, and remember that point as the next drag's origin, and `ZoomScroll` intercepts `ACTION_DOWN` when stopping a fling. An interruption ends momentum once, without a scroll-end event or a drag until the finger moves. A touch that stops a glide, fling or eased scroll is the view's, as in Android's own scroll views: the child under it never sees it, so lifting presses nothing and moving pans the view ([#358](https://github.com/nsgo-dev/nsgo/issues/358), [#1544](https://github.com/nsgo-dev/nsgo/issues/1544)).
- Overflowing single-axis scroll views on iOS and Android touch devices (phones and tablets, excluding tvOS, watchOS and web simulations) show a touch-draggable scroll handle along either vertical or horizontal axis (`internal/driver/scrollhandle.go` holds the shared geometry and scrub math; `internal/platform/ios/scrollhandle.go` places a hit-target overlay over `UIScrollView` with a zero-delay `UILongPressGestureRecognizer`; Android's `Scrolling.HandleView` draws and tracks touches on the scroll view's outer frame; and `nsgotest`'s `App.ScrollHandle` and `dragHandle` mirror it headless). Short scroll views that do not overflow, zooming views, `NoScroll`, `HideIndicator` and views with a custom `Scroll.Bar` or `ScrollbarTheme` hide the handle.
- Everything that scrolls has a touchpad's and a touchscreen's inertia on every target: the platform's own, or a glide where it gives none. iOS, Android and macOS scroll and glide as above and below; the web and Windows take the system's through the browser and the wheel messages it sends on; Linux glides itself from GTK's `decelerate` (in points per second though GTK documents milliseconds) on the surface and on `NoScroll` web views, and tracks surface-unit deltas in `scrollbar.Velocity` through the hosted-view shield until `gdk_scroll_event_is_stop` (staying latched if the hosted control scrolls out from under the pointer) so shielded touchpad scrolls glide the same way; fingers landing (`scroll-begin` or `GDK_TOUCHPAD_HOLD`) or a turn stop it.
- The web moves a scroll view by a wheel event's delta itself, as NSH's page does (`wheel.go`): the innermost view along the event's axis that can move that way takes it, a vertical wheel a horizontal view only where no vertical one can, and keeps it while events come within 200 ms. Notches glide a quarter of the way a frame, and the scroll ends 200 ms after the last. Views that snap are left to the browser's CSS snapping.
- A scroll on the web that starts below 40 points in pixels along its view's axis, as a touchpad's does, departs from NSH's: it is left to the browser, whose inertia, bounces and off-main-thread scrolling a page cannot match, and stays with it for the 200 ms rule above. Browsers let a page cancel a scroll's events only from its first, so this is decided there; macOS's mice, whose deltas are as small, scroll this way too.
- What a scroll past an edge does is the platform's: a rubber band on macOS and iOS, a stretch on Android (its scroll views' edge effect, a glow before Android 12), and a stop for touchpads on Windows and Linux. The web leaves it to the browser: its wheel handling takes only what can move, so a browser bounces where its platform does, nested views included (Safari, Firefox, Chrome from 145). `NoBounces` is `overscroll-behavior: none`.
- `ScrollBehavior.RefreshIndicator` is the height of an indicator the app draws itself, over the top of the scroll view, with styles bound to `PullValue`: the spinner is hidden, a release past that height refreshes, and refreshing keeps that room above the content (`RefreshThreshold`, `RefreshInset`; 64 and 56 points for the spinner). Platforms set the value bound with `AnimBindPull` to how far the content is pulled past its top, the room included, on their main thread. UIRefreshControl cannot give way to an indicator, so iOS then detects the pull as a drag ends and keeps the room with the top inset, as NSH's Mac idiom does.
- On iOS a scroll view's pan waits for the screen's swipe back to fail, so an edge drag goes back.
- Windows and Linux scroll with the wheel, the touchpad, the bar and touchscreen fingers (`scrollbar.TouchDrag`): a finger past the slop drags the innermost scroll view that moves its way, rubber-banding past the edges unless `NoBounces`, then glides by the deceleration, bouncing at the edge it reaches, snaps or springs back, and a pull past the top refreshes with the spinner or the app's indicator. The refresh threshold follows the finger's pull before rubber banding, so it does not vary with the viewport's size or `NoBounces`, unlike NSH's displayed-offset threshold. A finger landing, the app's `ScrollTo`, the wheel or the bar stops a glide or snap (`interruptScroll`), and a finger that brakes one keeps the touch as the scroll view's so lifting presses nothing while moving still drags it ([#1544](https://github.com/nsgo-dev/nsgo/issues/1544)). A wheel settles snapping views when it rests. The two backends share offset animation state and shift in-flight scroll endpoints and a grabbed thumb together when the engine anchors content with `ItemScrollBy`. Windows routes a wheel to the views along its axis, a vertical one to horizontal views only where none is vertical, as macOS does. They leave the keyboard props alone.
- Windows draws macOS's overlay indicator (`scrollbar.Bar.Indicator`): 6 points thick, rounded, 3 from the edge, fading 700 ms after the view or the pointer last moved and shrinking past the edges. Linux keeps its own bar, shown while the pointer is over the view.
- Windows and Linux retain the locally held refresh room when a configuration, content size or viewport changes, including a confirming commit that arrives after the refresh animation has ended. Keeping a resting view at its start includes that room; explicit scroll commands and reports to the engine still use ordinary content bounds.
- Android applies `KeyboardShouldPersistTaps` after a tap is dispatched, including a tap consumed by a child, on vertical, horizontal and zoomable scroll views. `KeyboardTapsHandled` keeps a handled row's keyboard, while `KeyboardTapsNever` dismisses it; taps on an input keep its keyboard.
- iOS and Android keep a focused field in a scroll view clear of the software keyboard: the keyboard's height joins the bottom inset while it shows, and the field scrolls into view with the keyboard's motion.
- Zooming (`MaxZoomScale` above `MinZoomScale`, `Ref.ZoomTo`) is iOS's zoom view, Android's `ZoomScroll`, a scaled content element on the web, the container's bounds on macOS, and scaled child transforms with Ctrl+wheel zooming and 2D panning on Windows and Linux (`scrollbar.ZoomEase`). Mouse drags never scroll content.
- On the web, a zoom's last step schedules canvas replay after 120 ms without another step, at the cumulative scroll zoom and style transforms clamped to 1–4 times the screen's pixel ratio, drawing only the visible slice when a full bitmap would exceed 4096 × 4096 pixels ([canvas](canvas.md)). A new command, a pinch or Ctrl+wheel cancels the previous zoom animation; changing the limits cancels it too, so an old target cannot override the new one. Deleting a scroll cancels both the animation and pending replay.

### Edge effects

`Scroll.EdgeEffects` sets the views over a scroll view's sides apart from the content scrolling under them, as iOS 26's scroll edge effects do: its bars, views whose `EdgeBar` names the scroll view by its `Ref` and a side. NSH has none; the looks follow iOS 26's and measures of macOS 27's.

- After layout the core measures how far each side's bars reach into the scroll view (`markEdgeScrolls`) and sends it in `ScrollConfig.Edges`, with the bars, the background the content scrolls over and a hard edge's hairline. Automatic is soft under bars and nothing without them.
- Both edges lay the background at 80% under the bars. A soft edge blurs most at the side, down to nothing 24 points past the bars, and its background eases out along smoothstep's curve from 24 points short of the bars' end, so no line shows where the fade begins; a hard edge frosts evenly under the bars, with a half-point hairline past them (`driver.SoftEdgeLevels`, `SoftTint`, `BandRect`, `HairlineRect`).

| Target | Draws |
| --- | --- |
| macOS | A `CABackdropLayer` over the content, under the scroll bar, as AppKit's own pockets (`NSScrollPocket`) draw: soft, Core Animation's `variableBlur`, whose radius follows a mask image's alpha from the side to nothing at the band's end (its radius spreads twice a Gaussian's, so it takes half the blur); hard, an even `gaussianBlur`. The tint and hairline are layers over it. Both classes are private, as `NSVisualEffectView` cannot vary its blur, and AppKit's own edge effect reaches only `NSScrollView`s under a title bar's accessories |
| iOS | As macOS, in a holder over the content that follows the offset inside the scroll view. `UIScrollEdgeEffect` stays hidden: its reach does not follow the bars, nor a screen's own `UINavigationBar`, so it stops short of a tall bar's end |
| Web | Bands that stick over the content (`position: sticky`, from the top left, as the content beside them is absolutely placed), stacking `backdrop-filter` blurs whose radii double toward the side, each fading in through `mask-image` |
| Windows | Over the children, from one copy of the target: each level blurred by its whole blur through Direct2D's Gaussian blur and faded in by a gradient opacity brush |
| Linux | The background alone, at 85% under soft bars, easing out as elsewhere, or at 95% under hard bars, as GTK draws nothing behind a widget |
| Android | From Android 12 (API 31), `ScrollEdges` records the scroll view's children into a `RenderNode` and draws each active band with `RenderEffect.createBlurEffect`: progressive masked blur levels (`SoftEdgeLevels`) under the smoothstep tint for a soft edge, or an even blur under the tint and hairline for a hard edge. Before Android 12 (or on software canvases), it falls back to the unblurred tint scrim and hairline |

### Bars of the app's

`Scope.SetScrollbars` has a `ScrollbarMaker` draw the bar of every scroll view, list and multiline `Input` that shows its indicator, and `Scroll.Bar`, `List.Bar` and `Input.Bar` one view's; `base.SetScrollbarTheme` and `base.ScrollbarTheme` are what apps use, TSX's `Scrollbar.setTheme` and `scrollbar={theme}`. As NSH's `core/scrollbars.rs`, the bar is a child of the view, last so it draws over the content and what comes later goes before it: in a scroll view, a host as large as the viewport pinned over it by a sticky range across all of the content, which the platforms apply on the main thread, so it never waits for a commit; in a multiline `Input`, a host laid out at the input's frame over its editor without making the input a non-leaf to Taffy's text measurement, and without scrolling with the text. The indicator goes (`HideIndicator`) while a bar stands in for it. The host stays once made, empty while nothing draws a bar; views that never have one pay nothing. A maker that returns nil, a theme with no look for the axis, keeps the platform's indicator.

`Scope.WatchScroll` reports a scroll view's, list's or multiline `Input`'s offset, viewport and content to a bar, or to anything else outside it, after each commit or event that moved them, from its first layout, naming it by a function that is read each commit, as a ref may be bound later in the same render. Every backend reports a multiline `Input`'s text offset (and scrollable height) on `EventScroll` and takes `ItemScrollTo`, so `Ref.ScrollTo`, `Ref.ScrollToEnd`, `Input.OnScroll` and dragging a `Scrollbar` thumb work over multiline inputs as they do over scroll views.

### macOS scroll views

macOS follows NSH's `scroll.rs` (`internal/platform/macos/scroll.go`, `wheel.go`); the rubber band, the indicator's geometry and fade, the trackpad's resampling and revealing are `internal/platform/scrollbar`'s.

- A scroll view is a plain view whose container holds and clips its children. A moving offset is the container layer's `sublayerTransform`, one layer write a frame. The container's bounds origin takes it 140 ms after the last move, once the frame clock rests or the view has rested for a second, since `setBoundsOrigin:` walks the whole subtree.
- AppKit's hit testing, conversions and tracking areas follow the bounds, so presses, mouse moves, a scroll gesture's start, selections, a focused field and the test host commit first. Accessibility frames catch up at the commit.
- A scroll puts off the pick of what came under a mouse that did not move, the views hovered and the cursor, until 120 ms after its last step, as NSH's `REPICK_MS`: a pick is a hit test of the surface, and a scroll moves content every frame. Batches and AppKit's cursor updates wait with it, and the pick commits first.
- Trackpad gestures rubber-band past the edges unless `NoBounces` and spring back; the system's momentum follows and bounces at the edge it reaches, as AppKit's does, where NSH's stops (`scrollbar.Bounce`). Their events are shown in step with the display, one event interval back, so no frame moves twice or not at all.
- Snapping views snap from the release, with `DecelerationRate` projecting it, instead of taking the system's momentum. Mouse wheels move 10 points a line, and snapping views settle once the notches stop.
- A gesture and its momentum stay on the view they began on, and wheel notches while they come within 200 ms and the pointer stays over it. AppKit latches a gesture to the view under the pointer, which in a virtualized view can unmount as it scrolls and take the rest of the events with it, so once a gesture has its view the window's event monitor hands them to it directly; NSH needs no monitor, as its root view takes every event. Views along the wheel's axis take it, and at the edges a gesture stretches the innermost view along it.
- The indicator shows while the user moves the view (the wheel, the trackpad and the system's momentum, the indicator dragged, a pinch, a selection dragged past the edge, and the snap or spring back after them) and while the pointer is over it (unless the system shows scroll bars only while scrolling); the app's scrolls (`ScrollTo`, `ScrollToEnd`, `ScrollBy`, a focused field revealed, a refresh's room) leave it as it is, as AppKit's `scrollToPoint:` does. It fades after 700 ms, shrinks past the edges, and the mouse drags it as a scroll bar. A press that stops a snap is no press.
- Pulling a refreshable view past its top shows the spinner's spokes as it goes, and changing `ContentInset` while resting at the refresh indicator retains that room (`scrollbar.RestInset`), as on Windows and Linux. A field that gets the focus scrolls into view, and a selection dragged past the edge scrolls on the frame clock.
- Offsets are reported within the content, as NSH's, once a frame.
- A zooming view (`zoom.go`) departs from NSH's, which ignores zooming: the container's bounds hold the zoom, so AppKit scales the content and hit tests it where it shows, and a new zoom commits at once. It moves along both axes, without resampling; trackpad pinches zoom around the pointer, past the limits unless `NoBouncesZoom`, and spring back.

## Lists

`List` follows NSH's `<List>` and its `ListState` (`liststate.go`). It mounts only the rows within a viewport of the visible ones, in absolutely placed cells it reuses: a cell's content is built once, and its `ListRow` signals move it to another row. Item sizes start as an estimate and are corrected as cells are laid out; rows (`NumColumns` items, as long as the longest) sit in a Fenwick tree, so offsets and the row at an offset cost O(log n). The window is refilled after each layout, within the same commit.

- With `Key`, a change of the keys is a splice by their common start and end: kept rows keep their sizes and cells.
- A spare cell runs nothing until it shows a row again, where NSH's keeps its last row: its index may name a row that is gone, and Go's slices panic past their end where JavaScript reads `undefined`. Its scope is parked (`parking` in `reactive.go`), so `runTop` holds back what runs under it and the cell runs it once with its new index as it is reused; a memo of the cell read from outside keeps its last value meanwhile, then runs for those readers too. The binding that follows `Count` and `Key` guards every cell: `runTop` brings it up to date before anything in a cell runs, so a row never sees an index its list has not settled, whatever order their subscriptions are in.
- A splice or a measurement wholly above the viewport keeps the visible rows in place with an `ItemScrollBy`, which platforms apply unclamped, carrying a drag, glide or animated scroll along.
- `Inverted` lays the content out from the end and keeps its distance from it; `MaintainVisibleContentPosition` follows the end of other lists within its threshold. `Alignment: ListAlignEnd`, GPUI's `ListAlignment::Bottom` and NSH's `alignment="bottom"`, rests a list at its end with its rows in reading order: an end pin from its first update, so it opens there before its first frame, the end followed within a pixel as the content grows or shrinks, since scroll views keep an offset past content that shrank, and content shorter than the viewport after a lead of room (`leadRoom`). The header, footer, empty view and separators (in the row's cell) are placed as rows are.
- Rows laid out since the last update are measured in any order, each anchored with the offset the ones before it left, so a row above the viewport keeps the visible rows in place whatever order the cells come in.
- When a keyed list prepends rows with the viewport starting in its header, measurements before the retained row keep it in place, even if the new rows overlap the viewport's start.
- `Ref.ListPosition` reads the row the viewport starts in, from the side row 0 is on, and how far into it, from the list's own row positions, as GPUI's `ListOffset` and NSH's `measureScrollPosition`: `ScrollToIndexAt(index, 0, -offset)` restores it in a list made anew, its pin following the row as it measures.
- The web applies absolute and relative scroll commands in their original order after the batch's content sizes, so the browser does not clamp list anchoring to the old extent.
- Platforms count the `ItemScrollTo` and `ItemScrollBy` items each scroll view applied and report the count in `Event.Count`; the core replays the scrolls a report predates, as NSH's `ScrollState::issued` does.
- `Ref.ScrollToIndexAt`, `ScrollToEnd` and `InitialScrollIndex` resolve with the sizes known and pin their target. The list follows it with an `ItemScrollBy` or a new animated `ItemScrollTo` as rows are measured, until the viewport's are or the user scrolls away.
- Sticky rows (one column, not inverted) are computed in the core and sent as `ItemStickyCells`: each cell's travel in offsets, which macOS, iOS, Windows, Linux and Android apply on every scroll. Sticky cells move to the end of their list's children, so they draw above the others.
- The web puts each sticky cell in a track spanning its travel and lets CSS `position: sticky` pin it, clipping views with `overflow: clip` where the browser has it.
- A list's `StickyHeaderOffsetValue`, like a sticky view's `AnimatedStyle.StickyInset`, pins its headers an animated node's value from the start, as under a header that hides; the backend pins them again as the node changes, without a commit. macOS, iOS, Windows and Linux share the cell placement and animated inset tracking in `scrollbar`. Each backend applies those shifts to its own views.
- `OnViewableItemsChange` reports rows by `ViewabilityConfig`'s thresholds, after `MinimumViewTime` on the graph's clock.
- `OnLoadMore` runs once per count as the viewport comes within `LoadMoreThreshold` viewports of the content's end, as the list opens too.
- `OnLoadMoreAtStart` is its counterpart at item 0's end, once per approach, and only after the user or a scroll command moved the list (`listState.moved`).
- With `OnLoadMoreAtStart`, an inverted list keeps its distance from the end only while row 0 shows. Scrolled away, a splice or a measurement before the viewport keeps the visible rows in place (`keepsVisibleRows`), so pages loaded there arrive out of sight.
- With `ListViewportParent` the list is a plain clipping view whose spacer gives it its full length in its parent's layout. It is virtualized against the nearest scroll view on its axis around it, else its root, modal, overlay or screen.
- Such a list's anchoring and scroll commands scroll that view, and its sticky rows go in that view's `ItemStickyCells`, the last stopping at the list's end. The scroll view props, `Inverted` and `MaintainVisibleContentPosition` are ignored; NSH warns of them, NSGo does not yet.

Binding is paced, as NSH's: a fast scroll or a jump asks for a screen of cells at once, and each bind builds or rebinds a cell's content.

- The viewport, twice the last scroll step ahead of it (none after a jump longer than the viewport) and the sticky rows bind in the commit that asks for them.
- The rest of the window waits and binds nearest the leading edge first, `paceCells` (4) a list per commit while the commit has run less than 2 ms, then on the next display frames (`Tree.Pacing`).
- A bound cell's views past a commit's first 64 are made over frames too, those in sight at once ([performance](performance.md#keeping-frames-cheap)).
